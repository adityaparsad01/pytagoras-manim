Why does a squared plus b squared equal c squared?

Let's see it instead of memorizing it.

Start with a right triangle. The two shorter sides are a and b, and the longest side, opposite the right angle, is c.

Now build a square on each side.

The area of a square with side a is a squared. The area on side b is b squared. And the area on the hypotenuse is c squared.

For a three, four, five triangle, those areas are nine, sixteen, and twenty-five.

Nine plus sixteen is twenty-five. But that is more than a numerical coincidence.

Here is the general idea.

Take a large square whose side is a plus b. Inside it, place four copies of the same right triangle. What remains in the middle is a square whose side is c.

The area of the large square is a plus b, all squared.

The same area can also be counted as the four triangles, plus the middle square.

The four triangles together have area two ab. The middle square has area c squared.

So we have a squared plus two ab plus b squared equals c squared plus two ab.

Subtract two ab from both sides.

And what remains is the Pythagorean theorem: a squared plus b squared equals c squared.

The picture is the proof.
