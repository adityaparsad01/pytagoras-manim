Why does a squared plus b squared equal c squared?

Let’s see it instead of memorizing it.

Start with any right triangle. The two shorter sides are a and b. The side opposite the right angle is the hypotenuse, c.

Here’s a three, four, five triangle. Build a square on each side. The three areas are a squared, b squared, and c squared.

For this example, that means nine plus sixteen equals twenty-five.

But why does this always work?

Take four identical copies of the same right triangle and place them inside one large square. The large square has side length a plus b.

That means its area is a plus b, squared: a squared, plus two a b, plus b squared.

The four triangles together have area two a b. What remains in the middle is a square with area c squared.

So the same total area can be written in two ways: a squared plus two a b plus b squared equals c squared plus two a b.

Now the two a b terms cancel.

And what remains is the Pythagorean theorem: a squared plus b squared equals c squared.

The geometry makes the equation inevitable.
